Method & limitations

1. Face & ROI tracking

MediaPipe FaceLandmarker locates 468 facial landmarks per frame. Forehead and both cheeks are used as regions of interest — skin patches relatively free of motion from talking or blinking.

2. Signal extraction

Every frame, the mean red/green/blue value of each ROI is sampled from the actual video pixels (not the landmarks themselves) and pushed into a rolling, timestamped buffer.

3. Resampling

The buffer is resampled onto a fixed-rate time grid — handling variable camera framerate — before any frequency-domain processing, since FFT assumes evenly spaced samples.

4. POS algorithm

Projects the normalized RGB signal onto a plane orthogonal to the expected skin-tone direction, canceling out most illumination and skin-tone variation and isolating the blood-volume pulse signal.

5. Frequency analysis

The pulse signal is detrended, windowed, zero-padded, and run through an FFT; the dominant frequency in the plausible heart-rate band (42–200 BPM) becomes the estimate. Signal quality is the ratio of power at that peak to the average power elsewhere in the band.

6. Breathing rate

Estimated the same way, but from the low-frequency vertical motion of the head (0.1–0.6 Hz / 6–36 breaths per minute) over a longer window, since breathing needs more cycles to resolve.

7. HRV

Beat-to-beat intervals come from peak detection on the bandpassed pulse waveform; RMSSD is computed from consecutive interval differences.

8. VitalSense accuracy fixes (this build)

Versus the PulseSight prototype this file was built from: motion gate uses mean (not summed) landmark displacement with a matching threshold, so a still face no longer reads as motion; breathing tracker uses mean face-Y instead of a geometrically wrong min/max mix; per-ROI quality gating drops dark/saturated patches before fusion; 1 s moving-average detrending + zero-phase brickwall bandpass precede the FFT; parabolic interpolation gives ~0.2 BPM resolution; an octave-jump guard (median-of-3, hold-last-value) stops >18 BPM flicker; HRV peaks come from the filtered waveform. The ASHA strip adds 30 s baseline calibration, stress label, 0–100% confidence, and a 10 s-persisted discomfort alert.

Known limitations

Motion sensitivity — talking, laughing, or head movement corrupts the signal.
Lighting sensitivity — very dim, flickering, or strongly colored lighting biases the RGB signal.
Skin tone & camera bias — published rPPG research has documented accuracy varying systematically across skin tones. This prototype has not been validated across a diverse population.
No ground-truth validation here — the algorithm was verified on synthetic signals with a known embedded frequency, confirming the math is correct, not that live camera accuracy matches a clinical device.
Not for arrhythmia or clinical HRV use — this pipeline assumes a roughly regular pulse and isn't designed to detect irregular heartbeats.
How it works: every heartbeat pushes a small pulse of blood into facial skin capillaries, causing a color change far too subtle to see directly. The POS algorithm (Wang et al. 2017) combines red, green, and blue channels of a tracked skin region to cancel out lighting and skin-tone effects and isolate that pulse signal, then an FFT finds its dominant frequency.
